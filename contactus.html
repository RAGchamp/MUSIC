Why Music, and Why AI?

Learning a song is harder than it should be. Sheet music has to be read, recordings go by too fast, and it is not obvious which note is being played, when, or with which finger. Teachers help, but not everyone has one, and notes for the songs people love (especially Bollywood songs) are often missing, paid, or wrong.

I am exploring how AI can lower that barrier so that more people can learn music easily: by finding the notes in a recording, reading them from sheet music, playing them back slowly, and showing them on a keyboard, a guitar fretboard, a staff and even two moving hands. The idea is simple: see it, hear it, slow it down, then play it.

What the App Does

🎶 Song PracticePlays the original recording and lights up each note on the piano or guitar as it sounds; slow it down to 50%. 🎹 Piano demoPress "Try it" and watch two hands play Für Elise or Pehla Nasha, with the score scrolling below. 🙌 Piano Notes PlayerLoad any notes file: hear it, see the fingers press the keys, and loop just the bars you are learning. 🎹 Mini PianoOne octave of big keys for your phone: tap a tune, or type keys and wait times in a table and let it play. 📝 Music Basics Test 125 questions on music theory: hear every answer on a mini piano, then get it marked with an explanation. 🎙️ RecorderRecord a song playing in another browser tab as a WAV file, and trim it to the part you want to learn. 🧪 Notes TesterPlays the written notes and compares them with the recording, line by line, to find the wrong ones. 🔬 WAV VisualizerTwo recordings side by side as coloured note boxes, with the differences outlined. 🎹 Piano CapturePlay along on your laptop keyboard and capture the exact notes and timings. 📘 How it worksStep-by-step explainers of the app, the note analysis and the Piano Notes Player.

Built Entirely with Claude Code

Every page of this app, and every tool behind it, was built with Claude Code, Anthropic's AI coding agent, working in my own folder on my own laptop. I did not hand-write the code. I described what I wanted in plain words (often with a screenshot), listened, tested, and said what to change. Claude Code wrote, ran and checked everything else.

🧑 What I did

  • Chose the songs, recorded clips and found the sheet music
  • Described each feature in everyday language, with screenshots
  • Listened: "it sounds like a spoon on a steel bowl", "the timing is off"
  • Decided the look and feel: colours, boxes, buttons
  • Tested every page and asked for fixes

🤖 What Claude Code did

  • Wrote every HTML, CSS and JavaScript page, with no build step
  • Analysed recordings with Python (pitch, note starts, tuning) to find the notes
  • Read 177 bars of Pehla Nasha from screenshots, and all of Für Elise from a PDF automatically
  • Worked out the hand split and fingering, and the soft piano sound
  • Tested in a headless browser, and wrote the "How it works" guides

Some examples of what that looked like in practice: a 73-second film clip turned into 246 timed notes; a five-minute piano arrangement read bar by bar and checked so that every bar adds up; a PDF of Für Elise converted into notes by code, with its repeats, triplets and "8va" passage handled; and a keyboard where two hands press the keys with numbered fingers while the score scrolls in step.

How You Can Use It, and Build on It

  1. Practise with it. Open the app in any browser, on a laptop or phone. No sign-up, nothing to install. Start with the Piano demo.
  2. Add your own songs. The whole app is plain files in a public repository, github.com/RAGchamp/MUSIC. Record a clip with the recorder page, put its notes in a text file (one line per note: time, notes, beats), and the practice pages, the Notes Tester and the Piano Notes Player can all use it.
  3. Teachers and learners: make a practice page for a student, slow down the hard bars, and loop just those bars until they feel easy.
  4. Build your own with Claude Code. You don't need to be a programmer to start. What worked for me:
    • Describe the result you want in plain words; paste a screenshot when it is easier to show than to tell.
    • Ask it to test its work (it can open the page in a browser and check it), and to tell you honestly what it could not check.
    • Work in small steps, listen to the result, and say exactly what is wrong. "Too shrill" is useful feedback.
    • Keep a short project notes file (Claude Code uses a CLAUDE.md) so it remembers your decisions from one session to the next.
    • Ask it to explain what it built: the "How it works" guides in this app came from exactly that.
A note on accuracy: this is a personal learning project. Notes found from recordings, the left/right hand split and the fingering are the app's best effort, not a teacher's or an arranger's. Please check against the music, trust your ears, and tell me when something is wrong. I would love to hear which songs you would like to learn next.
